Case Study · IoT / Edge

Building Structure Around Connected Systems

The hard part of IoT is rarely getting one device to work on a bench. The real challenge is making connected systems supportable across hardware, firmware, connectivity, field conditions, vendors, and handoff.

EnvironmentIoT / Edge / Field Operations
FocusLifecycle · Connectivity · Testing
Operating ModelDocumentation · Vendors · Handoff
ExamplesBLE · UHF · Edge · Remote Connectivity

The Situation

Connected systems were growing faster than the operating model around them.

The environment included customized devices, field deployments, wireless connectivity, edge components, vendor dependencies, and hardware changes that needed to move from prototype behavior into something operations could actually support.

Documentation was incomplete. Device variants, firmware considerations, bills of materials, test methods, and support knowledge were not always consolidated. That made troubleshooting slower and increased the risk that important information lived only in individual memory or vendor conversations.

What Needed Improvement

Lifecycle Visibility

Teams needed a clearer picture of device versions, components, firmware/configuration differences, support status, and what changed between one field unit and another.

Repeatable Testing

Prototypes and field changes needed test criteria that could be repeated, documented, and compared rather than judged only by whether a single demonstration appeared to work.

Operational Handoff

Engineering, vendors, field teams, and support needed a shared path from concept to deployment so the final system did not become an orphaned technical experiment.

Approach

Treat the device as one part of a larger operating system.

1. Build the System Map

I worked to connect the pieces that are often treated separately: hardware, firmware/configuration, power, connectivity, cloud or edge dependencies, field installation, support ownership, and vendor responsibility. The goal was to make the real system visible.

2. Make Testing Explicit

For wireless and hardware concepts, I focused on measurable behavior rather than demo success. That included signal thresholds, connection paths, power behavior, environmental constraints, and what should count as pass/fail under realistic field conditions.

3. Design for Field Constraints

Connectivity plans considered locations where normal infrastructure might not yet exist or might be unreliable. That pushed the design toward practical combinations of cellular, Wi-Fi, satellite, local edge processing, and independent power rather than assuming a perfect site.

4. Improve Handoff and Ownership

I pushed documentation, test notes, vendor decisions, and support knowledge toward a form that another person could actually use. The objective was to reduce hidden dependency on a single engineer, vendor contact, or undocumented configuration.

Representative Work

BLE Presence / Muster Concepts

Evaluated beacon-based presence concepts using multiple BLE beacons, Wi-Fi connectivity, edge processing, and signal-strength thresholds to reason about whether a person or asset was truly present in a defined area.

UHF and Identification Testing

Explored UHF-based identification and related gating concepts where the technical question was not simply “can it read?” but whether the read behavior was reliable enough for a real operational workflow.

Day-One Connectivity

Worked through connectivity concepts combining satellite service, independent power, and edge/network equipment to support sites before conventional infrastructure was fully available.

Power Continuity

Evaluated local backup-power concepts for connected devices where short interruptions could create avoidable outages, resets, or support events.

Device State / Check-Out Logic

Explored ways to determine whether devices were still physically present, checked out, or moved between expected areas using wireless signals and identity controls.

Hardware / Field Changes

Supported testing and operational thinking around physical hardware changes where electrical behavior, installation realities, documentation, and field support all had to converge.

Operating Principle

Prototype success is not operational success.

A connected-device project becomes real only when someone other than the original builder can deploy it, observe it, troubleshoot it, replace it, and understand what version they are dealing with.

That means lifecycle records, configuration visibility, support ownership, test criteria, and field documentation are not administrative overhead. They are part of the product.

What I’d Carry Forward

Start the operating model at the same time as the prototype.

I would establish the device registry, firmware/configuration map, bill of materials, test template, deployment checklist, and ownership matrix at the beginning of the project—not after the prototype proves interesting.

That shift turns IoT from a sequence of clever experiments into a manageable technology platform. It also creates a cleaner foundation for telemetry, automation, fleet management, security, and future AI-assisted operations.
